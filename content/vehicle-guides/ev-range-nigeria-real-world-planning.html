A catalogue range figure helps you begin a shortlist. It does not tell you, on its own, whether a vehicle can complete your delivery route, passenger schedule or intercity journey with a comfortable reserve. The useful question is how much energy your actual work needs between reliable charging opportunities.

Sure Imports displays supplier-stated range and the test standard where it has been provided. Treat missing test information as a question to resolve, not an invitation to assume the most favourable interpretation.

Compare like with like

Record the exact configuration, battery size and range label together. Two versions of the same model can have different batteries, seating or bodies. A refrigeration unit or passenger conversion can also change the job the vehicle is doing. A photograph of one version does not establish the performance of another.

Where figures use different test procedures, avoid treating the numbers as directly equivalent. Ask for the manufacturer’s test basis and conditions. There is no universal conversion percentage in this guide from a brochure figure to guaranteed Nigerian road performance.

What changes the energy needed for a route?

The US Energy Information Administration notes that temperature and energy used to maintain cabin and battery conditions affect range. It also describes differences between city and highway operation. These are general principles, not Ruichi-specific test results.

For your buying decision, record passenger or cargo load, distance, speeds, hills, air-conditioning use, stops and any powered equipment. A route name alone is insufficient. “Deliveries around Lagos” could describe very different distances, waiting times and daily loads.

Calculate a planning range from energy

Planning distance = energy allocated to the journey ÷ expected energy use per kilometre. Suppose, purely for illustration, you allocate 40 kWh to the planned route after setting aside your chosen reserve.

Choose a reserve that matches the consequences of delay

Set a reserve before assigning work to the vehicle. Consider diversions, queues, an unexpected extra drop and uncertainty about the next charger. A reserve is part of the operating plan, not unused capacity that should automatically be sold as additional route distance.

Ask what happens if the vehicle returns late or its intended charging location is unavailable. If that would leave passengers or goods stranded, build a practical contingency into the schedule. Check the actual charger and access arrangements rather than assuming a map marker guarantees a working session.

Collect a useful route diary before requesting a quote

  1. Record the start and finish location and daily distance for a representative week.
  2. Note the busiest day, not just the average.
  3. Record typical and maximum permitted loads you need to carry.
  4. Separate moving time, waiting time and time parked at a charger.
  5. List air-conditioning, refrigeration and other essential equipment.
  6. Mark which charging locations and hours you can actually rely on.

Send that diary with your enquiry. It allows the discussion to move from “Which has the biggest number?” to “Which configuration is suitable for this job?” Confirm payload as well as space: cubic metres of cargo volume do not establish how much weight a van may carry.

Validate your assumptions after delivery

Begin with manageable routes and collect consistent records of distance, load and charging. Compare similar operating days and investigate changes rather than interpreting one unusual trip as the vehicle’s permanent range. Use the manufacturer’s guidance for interpreting the battery display.

For a fleet, review whether the vehicle completes its assigned work and returns with the planned reserve. A model that fits a shorter urban round may still be the wrong choice for a second shift or a heavier route. Adjust assignments before expanding the fleet.
