## Email 2: The biggest beginner mistake

Subject: The biggest mistake new importers make

Preview: It happens before payment.

Body:
The biggest mistake is choosing a product because it looks cheap online.

A cheap product can still fail if nobody wants it, if quality is poor, if shipping is expensive, if accessories are missing, or if competitors already sell it better.

Before buying, ask: Who will buy this? Why will they choose mine? What quality level is acceptable? What is the total cost after shipping and local expenses? Can I sell fast enough to recover cash?

The product decision is more important than the supplier search. Before you contact any supplier, speak to at least five likely buyers or test the offer with a small audience. Questions, deposits and genuine price enquiries are stronger evidence than likes.

## Email 4: Compare product ideas before spending

Subject: Compare product ideas before you spend

Preview: Do not fall in love with one product too early.

Body:
Before importing, compare at least three product ideas.

Score each one on demand, competition, shipping weight, damage risk, warranty risk, selling price, repeat purchase potential, and how easy it is to explain to customers.

A product with lower profit per unit but faster turnover may beat a “high margin” product that sits unsold. A light product may beat a bulky one if shipping cost is the difference between profit and loss.

Good importers compare options before they commit cash. Give every idea a score from 1 to 5 for demand, competition, shipping, defect risk, cash required and expected selling speed. The numbers do not make the decision for you, but they expose weak assumptions.

## Email 5: MOQ without mistakes

Subject: MOQ is not always fixed

Preview: Minimum order quantity can often be negotiated.

Body:
MOQ means minimum order quantity. It tells you the smallest quantity a supplier prefers to produce or sell.

Beginners often see MOQ and give up. But MOQ can sometimes be negotiated, especially if you are willing to pay a sample fee, accept available stock, reduce customization, or start with a trial order.

Do not ask “Can you reduce MOQ?” only. Ask: “What is the smallest paid trial order you can support without customization?” That question sounds more serious.

When reviewing a ready-made supplier report, use its product-fit and buyer-note sections to prepare MOQ questions; do not assume every supplier in a category will accept the same trial quantity.

## Email 7: Manufacturer vs trading company

Subject: Manufacturer or trading company?

Preview: Both can be useful, but know the difference.

Body:
A manufacturer produces goods. A trading company sources and sells goods from factories. One is not always better than the other.

Factories can give better control, but may require higher MOQ. Trading companies can be flexible, but pricing and product control may vary.

Ask direct questions: Do you manufacture this product yourself? Where is the factory? Can you show production photos or videos? Can the invoice name match the company you are dealing with?

The goal is not labels. The goal is clarity.

If you want an example of how manufacturer evidence, product fit and contact attribution can be recorded, compare the structure of a product-specific supplier report with the claims made in a marketplace listing.

## Email 8: Supplier verification basics

Subject: How to verify a supplier before paying

Preview: Verification reduces risk; it does not remove it.

Body:
Supplier verification starts with identity.

Ask for the Chinese company name, Chinese address, website, contact person, phone, business license, invoice details, and bank/payment details. Compare these details. If the names do not match, ask why.

Also check whether the supplier can answer product-specific questions. A real supplier should understand materials, MOQ, production time, packaging, warranty and export requirements.

Never rely on one signal. Use several checks together.

Use a three-part check: identity, capability and transaction. Identity asks whether the legal company, address and payment beneficiary match. Capability asks whether the supplier can actually make the exact product. Transaction asks whether the quotation, invoice, specifications and payment instructions describe the same deal.

## Email 14: Landed cost

Subject: Calculate landed cost before importing

Preview: Profit begins before the goods move.

Body:
Landed cost means the total cost of getting goods from supplier to your selling point.

It includes product cost, China-side charges, shipping, clearing, local delivery and other direct costs. You should also leave room for defects, returns and price changes.

Do not wait until goods arrive before calculating. If landed cost is too high, you need to change product, quantity, shipping method, supplier or selling price.

Landed cost is where many import mistakes become visible.

## Email 17: Cheap shipping risk

Subject: Cheap shipping can become expensive

Preview: Low price is not the only question.

Body:
Cheap shipping can be useful, but ask what is included.

Does it include pickup? China warehouse handling? Customs process? Delivery location? Insurance or claim route? What happens if goods are delayed, damaged or misdeclared?

The cheapest quote can become expensive if communication is poor, documentation is weak, or hidden charges appear later.

Choose shipping based on clarity, not price alone.

Compare quotations on the same basis: chargeable weight or CBM, route, included handling, customs assumptions, delivery point, transit range and exclusions. Two prices are not comparable when one ends at the port and the other includes local delivery.

## Email 20: Avoid underpricing

Subject: Many importers underprice without knowing

Preview: Selling price must cover more than goods.

Body:
Underpricing happens when you calculate only product cost and shipping.

Your selling price should also consider defects, discounts, delivery, payment charges, marketing, packaging, storage, time, and the next restock.

If you sell too cheaply, you may move stock but still weaken your business. Profit must support replacement inventory.

The goal is not just to sell out. The goal is to sell and have enough cash to buy again.

Subtract every expected cost from the proposed selling price—not only product and freight. Then ask whether the amount left can absorb discounts, a few defects and the next restock. If one small problem removes the entire profit, the price is too fragile.

Defect policy should be discussed before payment, not after anger.

Put the remedy in writing. Define how defects will be counted, the evidence deadline, whether the solution is replacement, spare parts or credit, and when it will be delivered. Keep samples, inspection records and batch photographs so the discussion is based on evidence.
